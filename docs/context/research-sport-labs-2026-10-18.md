## The question

The owner: improve the fitness offering by looking at what the top 10–20 sport science labs
publish, on the belief that *"people are for sure trying to improve the quality of sport done
well by average people"*. Asked of the work: research by school (2022–2026), aimed at untrained,
recreational, busy or older adults, not elite sport — and, after the Norwegian 4×4 lesson
(`outstanding.md` §2.26), searched both by school and by the names people follow.

## What the research says, in the owner's terms

The readers found a lot on *how little is enough* and *how to keep at it*, little on *doing an
exercise well*. Across 21 schools, nothing turned up on form or technique feedback for
non-experts, on the talk test, or on wrist heart-rate accuracy. The one exception is gait
retraining in runners (Ghent, below). The labs studying "quality" are mostly studying dose and
adherence.

1. **Short and hard counts, if it is hard.** Vigorous bursts of 1–2 minutes in daily life
   (stairs, a fast uphill walk, carrying shopping) — *VILPA* — about 3 a day and 3.4–4.4 min/day
   in all, go with 26–49% lower mortality in people who do no exercise (Stamatakis, *Nat Med*
   2022, Sydney; *source*, observational). It takes about 45–80 s of a daily-life task to become
   vigorous (Ahmadi, *MSSE* 2024; *re-read, corrected*: n=70). A meta-analysis of 11 small RCTs
   of *exercise snacks* found a large effect on fitness, g=1.37, moderate certainty, and **none**
   on strength, body composition, blood pressure or lipids (Rodriguez et al. 2026, SDU co-author;
   *re-read*). In primary care, adherence to "snacks" was 32% (Daley, Loughborough 2025;
   *re-read*; "Snacktivity" is trademarked).
2. **Stopping short of failure is enough.** Training to failure adds little to muscle size and
   nothing clear to strength (Refalo 2023, Deakin, ES 0.19, CI touching zero; Robinson 2024;
   Hermann 2025 — one set to failure vs 2 in reserve, trained lifters, similar strength; Grgic
   2022, Victoria University; all *re-read* except Grgic). People's guess of "reps left" got
   better with practice — **in trained lifters**. This backs the ACSM 2026 line the app already
   quotes but cannot measure.
3. **Steps: there is a floor and a plateau.** Risk falls from about 2,500–2,800 steps a day,
   with most of the gain by 7,000–9,000 (Stens 2023, Granada; del Pozo Cruz 2024, Sydney; Ding
   2025, Deakin and the Norwegian School — the one already shipped). Among people under 8,000 a
   day, steps taken in walks of 10–15 minutes or more went with much lower risk than steps in
   bouts under 5 minutes (del Pozo Cruz 2025; *re-read*). All observational.
4. **Breaking up sitting moves blood pressure a little; advice alone barely moves sitting.**
   Coached stand-ups (+26 a day) cut diastolic pressure by about 2 mmHg (Hartman, *Circulation*
   2025, Deakin; *re-read*). Activity monitors add about 1,200 steps a day and cut sitting by
   about 10 min/day (Larsen, *BMJ* 2022, SDU; *re-read*). Advice alone did not change sitting
   (Biddle 2025, Loughborough).
5. **The gap is intention, not knowledge.** About half of people who mean to be active are not
   (Feil, Fritsch and Rhodes, *BJSM* 2023, Victoria CA; *re-read*). The things that close it in
   the reviews are habit, a fixed time and cue, and if-then plans (Rhodes 2022; Schumacher 2023,
   small; Hagger and Hamilton, Griffith). Gains fade when a programme stops (McEwan 2022).
6. **Simple self-tests predict long-term health.** A faster timed 5× chair-stand goes with lower
   odds of disability, depression, dementia and type 2 diabetes (Marín-Jiménez, *BJSM* 2026,
   Granada, 94 cohorts; *re-read*; very low to moderate certainty, an association not a test
   result).
7. **Strength work during weight loss keeps function.** With dieting, supervised high-intensity
   resistance and impact training improved gait speed, chair-stand and grip in older adults
   with obesity (Mesinovic 2025, Griffith/Deakin; *re-read*). After a diet, exercise kept the
   weight off and fitness up, alone or with a GLP-1 drug (Copenhagen, 2024 and 2026).
8. **Heavy lifting at retirement lasts.** One year of supervised heavy training at about 71
   held leg strength for four years while the control groups lost it (LISA, Copenhagen 2024;
   *re-read*; a secondary outcome, the primary not significant).
9. **Wearable VO2max is ±10 for one person.** Estimates from exercise data are unbiased on
   average, but one reading can be off by about 10 mL/kg/min; resting estimates are worse
   (Molina-García, *Sports Med* 2022, Granada; *re-read*).
10. **Running more softly can be taught.** Music-based feedback lowered tibial shock in
    runners, and an instructed 10-week start-to-run programme changed duty factor (Ghent, 2022
    and 2026; *abstract*, **not re-read**). This is the only technique work found, and it needs a
    sensor.

## Protocols people follow that the readers missed

The checker found these by name (the both-ways rule); *abstract* unless stated:

- **FIFA 11+ / "The 11"** warm-up — Oslo Sports Trauma Research Center, Norwegian School
  (Soligard, *BMJ* 2008): injuries RR 0.68 in youth football; the primary outcome was not
  significant. For football; FIFA's materials carry their own rights.
- **Osteo-cise: Strong Bones for Life** — Deakin (Gianoudis, *JBMR* 2014): community resistance,
  impact and balance training; function improved, falls did not.
- **LIFTMOR / Onero** — Griffith (Watson, *JBMR* 2018): twice-weekly supervised heavy lifting
  for women with low bone mass, 92% compliance. Sold as Onero; reuse terms unknown.
- **Not found, not claimed:** NTNU's fitness calculator / fitness age paper; Copenhagen and SDU's
  *Football Fitness* health sources; Granada's ALPHA-FIT battery. The register's own unread
  names (fartlek, Maffetone, 80/20, REHIT, run-walk, radio calisthenics) were not searched.

  it to the register.
